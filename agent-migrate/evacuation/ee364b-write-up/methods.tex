\section{Solving the Program}
\label{sec:methods}

Queue-Haul (Fig.~\ref{fig:sites}) solves the staged program as a short
lexicographic stack (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Each stage is a small convex program.
Two implementation details, backend choice and preconditioning, are worth stating
before we turn to the dual structure, which is where the payoff lies.

\paragraph{Backends.} The proportional-fair Stage~1 variant carries a log-utility
$\sum_q n_q\log(1-z_q/n_q)$ whose gradient blows up as $z_q\!\to\!0$, so it needs a
conic solver and tight tolerances. We build every stage in
CVXPY~\cite{diamond2016cvxpy}: the pure-LP stages use an LP solver, while that
Stage~1 variant and the Stage-2b QP go to the conic solver Clarabel~\cite{clarabel}.
A loose tolerance on the log-utility lets the iterate drift below the utility floor
and breaks the certificate handed to later stages.

\paragraph{Preconditioning.} The raw coefficients span about twenty orders of
magnitude: network terms are bytes
($\beta_q T_q,\,\eta_q T_q\sim10^{8\text{--}11}$), while prefill terms are
GPU-seconds ($T_q/\rho_q\sim10^0$). Left unscaled, this spread wrecks the
conditioning of both the LP and any first-order method. We normalize every capacity
row to $O(1)$. Dividing $L_i\le C_i$ by $C_i$ turns it into $p_i\le1$ with unit
right-hand side, and writes the whole program directly in the pressures $p_i$ it
already uses. This one rescaling is also what makes the first-order methods below
even tractable.

\paragraph{Lexicographic linking.} Each stage re-solves the full program with the
earlier optima frozen in. Stage 2 adds $\sum_q z_q=Z^\star$, Stage 3 adds that plus
$p_i\le\phi^\star$ for all $i$, and so on. The one subtlety is the proportional-fair
certificate. We carry it as $U(z)\ge U^\star-\delta$ with a small $\delta$, so the
floor survives finite solver accuracy instead of being tripped by a rounding-level
slack.

\subsection{The Stage-2 dual}

Stage 2 is where the duality is richest, because its peak-pressure objective turns
the multipliers into congestion prices. Dualize its pressure ceilings
$p_i(x)\le\phi$ with multipliers $\pi_i\ge0$. The Lagrangian is
$\phi\big(1-\sum_i\pi_i\big)+\sum_i\pi_i\,p_i(x)$, so minimizing over the free
$\phi$ forces
\[
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\pi_i=1:
\]
the prices live on a simplex. With $\phi$ gone the inner problem is linear in $x$
and \emph{separates by class}---each class sends its $n_q$ jobs to whichever
$(\ell,a)$ is cheapest at the current prices,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{cost}^R_{q\ell}(\pi)&=\pi^{\mathrm{net}}_\ell\tfrac{\beta_q T_q}{C^{\mathrm{net}}_\ell}+\pi^{\mathrm{pfill}}_{\ell m}\tfrac{T_q/\rho_q}{C^{\mathrm{pfill}}_{\ell m}},\\
\mathrm{cost}^S_{q\ell}(\pi)&=\pi^{\mathrm{net}}_\ell\tfrac{\eta_q T_q}{C^{\mathrm{net}}_\ell}+\pi^{\mathrm{ing}}_{\ell m}\tfrac{\eta_q T_q}{C^{\mathrm{ing}}_{\ell m}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
So $\pi_i$ is the marginal congestion price of resource $i$---how much the peak
moves if that resource is squeezed. CVXPY returns these prices exactly as the
ceiling duals, and they are interpretable on their own: they name which resource
binds, and by how much.

\paragraph{An envelope-theorem check.} The prices also predict sensitivity. Every
capacity is linear in the deadline, $C_i\propto D$, so the envelope identity
$\partial\phi^\star/\partial C_i=-\pi_i$ composes to
\[
\frac{d\phi^\star}{dD}=-\frac{1}{D}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\pi_i\,\hat p_i,
\]
with $\hat p_i$ the realized pressures. This is a testable prediction built from a single
LP solve. Sec.~\ref{sec:results} shows it lands on the finite-difference slope of
$\phi^\star(D)$ wherever the program is interior.

\subsection{First-order methods: a status report}

The per-class separation invites solving Stage 2 by ascending the dual instead of
calling a general LP solver. We implemented three textbook methods and race them
against the CVXPY $\phi^\star$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:convergence}). \emph{Projected
subgradient} takes a Polyak step on $(\pi,\nu)$ and projects $\pi$ onto the simplex
($\nu$ is the multiplier on the throughput link $\sum_q z_q=Z^\star$).
\emph{Entropic mirror descent}~\cite{beck2003mirror} swaps the Euclidean projection
for an exponentiated-gradient update---the natural geometry for the simplex, and a
better match to the $\ell_\infty$ peak. \emph{ADMM}~\cite{boyd2011admm} splits the
per-class assignments from the shared peak in a sharing form, alternating tiny
per-class QPs with a prox-of-max.

None is competitive yet. At the end of their budget the median relative gap to
$\phi^\star$ is still about $0.6$ (subgradient), $0.24$ (mirror descent), and above
$1$ (ADMM). The \emph{ordering} matches theory, in that mirror descent beats
subgradient, but the accuracy does not. ADMM is the most expensive method per
iteration, since each iteration solves a small QP per class; we run it for fewer
iterations than the others, so its curve is shorter and it remains far from
$\phi^\star$. Three things hurt. First, the objective is non-smooth: it is a max over
pressures, so the subgradient is one active constraint and the iterate chatters.
Second, the Polyak step starves when the throughput link is slack and $\nu$ rests at
zero. Third, even after normalization, one global step size fits the per-class costs
poorly. Averaging, restarts, and a diagonal price preconditioner are the next moves.
For now CVXPY is the solver, and the first-order methods are a study of the dual's
geometry.
